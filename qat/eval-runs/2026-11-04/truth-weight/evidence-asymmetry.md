## Positions
**A (steelman).** Hand antisepsis is a cheap, direct barrier against transferring pathogens to open wounds, and the effect shows up wherever it has been introduced: sharp falls in puerperal mortality in the Vienna clinics after chlorinated-lime handwashing, and its standing as a core element of modern perioperative infection-prevention guidance. **B (steelman).** Postoperative infection has many causes (patient condition, wound class, procedure length, antibiotics, air handling), so an individual practice could be statistically swamped; surgeons who skipped washing and saw no infections show that the link is not deterministic. The two do conflict directly on one empirical question: does the practice change infection rates on average. They share one premise: infections have multiple causes. That premise is compatible with A, since A claims a reduction, not sole causation.

## Framing notes
- "Makes no difference" is a strong universal-negative; B would need to defend it against every setting, whereas A needs only an average reduction.
- B's anecdotes confuse "not deterministic" with "no effect".
- The Vienna records are one setting, with possible confounders, so the historical row should not be read as proof by itself.
